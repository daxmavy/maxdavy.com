*Last updated 2026-09-12*

I studied a **Bachelors of Science (Advanced Mathematics)**, double-majoring in Mathematics and Computer Science at the University of Sydney, including an **Honours year in Applied Mathematics** (translation for non-Australians: this is an additional research year tacked onto the end of an undergraduate degree). My Honours thesis was on Discrete Morse Theory.[^morse]

I then worked for ~5 years, first at Atlassian (in their Ecosystem team), and then at Canva (in their Trust & Safety team). I had a great time and learned a lot about how to solve real-world problems with data at scale, and how to get stuff done.

However, the academic itch never left, so I studied a **MSc in Social Data Science** at the University of Oxford. I was lucky enough to get into the **DPhil in Social Data Science**, supported by the [Shirley Scholarship](https://www.oii.ox.ac.uk/about/giving/the-shirley-scholars-fund/) (from the OII) and the [BOLD Lab](https://bold-lab.ai/). I will start in Oct 2026, and am part of Exeter College.

Effective altruism has influenced me for a long time, primarily because it takes seriously the imperative for people to do good with their life (especially those with more resources), and to try to do so in a carefully considered manner. I think AI is likely to be transformative. It is important for it to go well, and I am concerned that capability gains are outstripping safety gains. 

Some quick takes:
- My theory of change is that independent[^labs] and high-quality safety research and evaluations can a) make a co-ordinated slowdown more likely, b) make AI safer, and c) improve the quality and efficacy of regulations.
- I do not think that addressing already-realised harms and possible future harms are mutually exclusive or in conflict except in some rare edge cases.
- I think that the 'we are in a great power struggle with China and thus need to kick off the arms race and throw safety to the wind' argument is dangerous for many reasons, and I have been quite shocked by the seeming consensus among American elites (of all political persuasions) on this point.
