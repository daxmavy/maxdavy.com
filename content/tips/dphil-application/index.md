*Last updated 21/11/26. If there’s something you want to know which this page doesn’t address, or if you want me to elaborate on a point, or if you have feedback, please contact me and I will happily address your question\!*

These points are completely unvalidated (I have no insider knowledge from the faculty members who actually make these decisions).

1. The OII loves interdisciplinarity and actively looks for it in students. If you feel like you find it difficult to place your research into a single discipline, that is good. If you are able to express this in the application in a non-bullshit manner, do that.  
2. Try to reach out to supervisors to develop an application together. As far as I can tell, it is a necessary requirement that a professor is excited about your project or your interest area, and new students’ research typically maps closely to the current interests of faculty members. Supervisors at the OII have dramatically diverse and different interests and worldviews. As such, it is likely that at most 1-3 supervisors might be suitable for you. Connecting with supervisors is thus **not** a volume game and it is not useful to spam them.   
3. Several faculty members at the OII do not reliably respond to emails from students outside of Oxford. Some do not reliably respond to emails from students inside of Oxford. I do not know any secret method to getting an academic to respond to you. Probably some necessary requirements are a) genuinely being interested in their line of research and demonstrating that with your initial questions, b) having something in your background which is ‘eye-catching’ to the faculty member. One important note: researchers’ interests change over time and it is likely that even their most recently-published works (which were probably initiated at least a year ago\!) do not correlate to their current interests. I recommend looking at the research pages of academics’ current PhD students, and also reaching out to PhD students, who are generally more willing and available to respond. For the love of all that is good in the world, do not use AI to mass-produce these emails.  
4. I have no clue what they use the writing sample for and how it is included in the evaluation. My CV is predominantly that of a data scientist and my undergrad degree was entirely mathematics. I decided that I should provide evidence that I could write decently on a non-quantitative topic. I thus did an analysis of a particular part of the *Digital Services Act*, a bit of European legislation that I had interacted with previously in my work. I have heard that it is a good idea to use the writing sample as evidence of areas where your application might otherwise look weaker in this way.   
   1. I think that in the writing sample, it is wise to have a narrow but deeply-researched argument, rather than general vibes-based writing as one might do in a blog. Can you summarise the argument and point of your paper in a single sentence? If not, it is probably not narrow enough.  
5. AFAIK, it is more common for an proposal to fail because it is too vague and ambiguously scoped than because it is too tightly or narrowly scoped. Whatever you’ve written, you should probably make the scope and research questions tighter. This shows a) your ability to define narrow research questions, of the sort that you address in a single research article, and b) that you recognise the time-limited nature of the DPhil.  
6. If you cannot answer the question ‘so what?’ for your research proposal (and especially for individual papers in your proposal) in a single sentence, your proposal almost certainly needs to change. A clear reason why the research matters is important.   
   1. Relatedly, the OII loves media attention. For many applicants, I suspect that a good heuristic is: would the findings from this research make an attention-grabbing Guardian headline \[while still being good science\]? Think of how you’d ‘market’ your research.  
7. People much wiser than me have written better things on how to come up with ideas for research projects. One important one: don’t do boring stuff that is similar to what’s been previously done. The worst way to do this is to look for a ‘research gap’ and fill it. Try to find a problem that hasn’t yet been addressed by anyone, or which is just emerging, or where standard approaches to analysing the problem completely fail for some interesting and generalisable and consequential reason.  
8. My format for my proposal document was to pitch 3 ideas for research articles: motivation, research question(s), methodology and expected contribution for each one.  
9. To write proposals for research articles, read lots of research articles and try to develop a sense of what constitutes a ‘good’ research article.   
10. If you get into the DPhil, you are not held to actually do what you proposed in your proposal. I have only met one current student (out of more than 10\) whose DPhil research vaguely resembles their proposal. You still need to treat it seriously for the purposes of the application, but don’t worry that you might commit to a topic that you later no longer like.   
    1. You do need to make sure that your interests line up with your supervisors’; and this is an important part of the DPhil proposal. If you have similar overall themes, motivations and methodologies, then I think it’s probably a good-enough fit.  
11. References: in general, academics are better than professional references, **IF** you have done a research project with the academic. I get the impression that just having done a class with an academic is not good evidence (unless the class was incredibly famously difficult, and you ranked first in the class and were amazing, and the professor will speak to all this, in which case I would be surprised that you did not go on to do research with that professor\!).  
12. Strongly consider applying to the MSc, rather than the DPhil. This is what I did, even though I knew pretty much 70% of the taught component of the MSc. I think that there is no chance that I would have gotten into the DPhil program without the MSc. Obviously the MSc is extremely expensive without funding, and not everyone has access to potential external funding sources, but I recommend this approach. Many current DPhil students previously did an MSc at the OII. I’d guess that doing a MSc gives a significant advantage when it comes to applying for OII DPhil programs \- comparable to publishing multiple good research papers or doing well at a fancy undergrad program or having a fancy job in policy or industry on your CV.

In case you’re not aware, funding is the ‘final boss’ for Oxford DPhils. Without funding, you will pay fees (35k pounds / year if you’re not a UK resident) for the pleasure of doing a Phd on top of your living costs in an expensive city. It is not *administratively* necessary to get funding to do the DPhil and do amazing research, but for many applicants getting funding is a necessary requirement to actually be able to study. Unfortunately, getting funding is hard, especially if you are not coming from a previous degree at Oxford or the OII.

Funding works like this, from what I know (this may be dramatically incorrect\!\!\!\!). There are five main sources of funding: external, research council/doctoral training programs, studentships, University-wide and department. 

1. External funding is any scholarship that has a separate application process. Many countries have their own funding programs, including some that are specific to Oxford. Get your favourite AI agent to have a look at options for you.  
2. The main research council funding for OII students is [Digital Social Science \| Grand Union Doctoral Training Partnership](https://www.granduniondtp.ac.uk/digital-social-science). There is an additional (and time-consuming) application form for it.  
3. Studentships are specific scholarships that professors are giving out from their own pots of money; you can find this on professors’ websites. They are not common in the OII.  
4. University-wide funding: despite the name, from what I know, the department needs to nominate people to University-wide funding. Within this subset, I think that several spots on the Clarendon scholarship are actually effectively decided by the department (I thiiiink??). Then, there is a wider pool of university-wide funding spots (in the Clarendon and other college-specific ones) that is more of a battleground between departments to get, and the chances of getting one of these is low.  
5. There is a small pool of money that the OII controls for scholarships (the Shirley Scholarships).

For my funding, I got a Shirley Scholarship. As far as I can tell, this means that I ranked relatively highly out of all the people who failed to get any other source of funding. This is not a complete scholarship, and only pays for my fees. I was then able to get a studentship for my living costs from one of my co-supervisors (who is not in the OII).
